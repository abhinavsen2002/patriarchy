## PART 2 — WHAT IS A PATRIARCHY

**[VISUAL]** Reddit fades. Clean white background. Two job candidates appear — identical in every visible way, except one wears a blue suit, one wears pink.

**[NARRATION]** "Before we answer that, we need to understand what a patriarchy actually is. Because most people's mental model of it is wrong."

**[NARRATION]** "Think about the colour pink. Is there anything wrong with pink? Of course not. But wear it to a job interview in many industries and people will notice. Wear blue, and nobody bats an eye."

**[VISUAL]** The blue-suited candidate moves through an interview smoothly. The pink-suited one gets slightly hesitant looks — nothing overt, just a pause.

**[NARRATION]** "Nobody in that room is thinking: I am discriminating against people who like pink. They're not thinking about it at all. But the effect is real."

**[NARRATION]** "Blue isn't more professional by any objective measure. It just happened to be what the powerful group was wearing when the norms were set. Once a trait is normalised by a dominant group, it gets rewarded — not because it's better, but because it's familiar. And this is how patriarchy works at its deepest level. Not through conscious discrimination, but through normalisation."

**[VISUAL]** The suits dissolve into abstract trait bars. Two columns appear:

```
"Male-coded"              "Female-coded"
Financial ambition   $    Nurturing
Risk tolerance       $    Collaboration
Assertiveness        $    Emotional intelligence
```

Dollar signs appear beside the left column. The right column has no markers.

**[NARRATION]** "Financial ambition isn't obviously more valuable than nurturing. Risk tolerance isn't obviously more important than collaboration. These traits just happened to be what the dominant group had when the economy was being built. And so they became what success looks like. The trait being rewarded might be completely arbitrary — like a colour preference. Which is exactly why people don't even realise when they're perpetuating it."

---

**[VISUAL]** Cut to a university campus. Two figures — M and F — start college at the same point, equal footing.

**[NARRATION]** "And it doesn't stop there. Networks matter."

**[NARRATION]** "M joins the football team. He makes a lot of friends. They bond over shared interests that F was gradually steered away from — not by anyone in particular, just by accumulated signals she absorbed growing up. She could have played football. But somewhere along the way she learned, without anyone explicitly telling her, that it wasn't quite her game."

**[VISUAL]** M's social web expands — a large, densely connected blue network. F's network grows too — smaller, separate.

**[NARRATION]** "Ten years later, most of M's friends are in senior positions at major companies. Not because they're more talented. Because networks compound. They make introductions. They create opportunities. F's network is real and meaningful — but smaller, and less embedded in the economy. Through no fault of anyone's."

**[NARRATION]** "In this world, everyone is equal. Some are just more equal than others."

---

**[VISUAL]** Both networks fade. Screen clears.

**[NARRATION]** "So here is the question we actually want to answer. What if we built a world with absolutely none of this. No normalisation of male traits. No network exclusion. No overt discrimination. Men and women are identical in every way that matters. Nobody even knows what gender is. Can this world still become a patriarchy?"

**[VISUAL]** Clean text fades in, then out: *Let's find out.*

## PART 3 — SIMULATION SETUP

**[VISUAL]** Black screen. A single user icon appears. Then another. Rapid succession — 100 icons scatter across the screen.

**[NARRATION]** "We are going to build a tiny society. One hundred people."

**[VISUAL]** All icons simultaneously morph into simple circular dots. Same positions.

**[NARRATION]** "I'm not going to keep drawing people, so let's just use dots."

**[VISUAL]** Zoom into one dot. A profile card expands. Ten horizontal bars appear one by one, each a different length.

**[NARRATION]** "Each person has ten attributes. Skills, interests, personality traits — for simplicity, just numbers. But not all of them matter equally."

**[VISUAL]** Attributes 1, 2, 3 brighten. A small currency icon appears beside them. Attributes 4–10 fade to low opacity.

**[NARRATION]** "Society only rewards three of them. The other seven still exist. They just don't affect your income."

**[VISUAL]** Zoom out. Every dot gets a floating score. Dots rearrange into a vertical leaderboard, highest to lowest.

**[NARRATION]** "We rank everyone by those three attributes. That ranking determines your job. Hiring is perfectly meritocratic — no one looks at anything except the score."

**[VISUAL]** Three job containers appear. Nodes flow into them.

**[TEXT ON SCREEN]**
```
Job A  — 15 slots  — Pay: 1000
Job B  — 35 slots  — Pay: 300
Job C  — 50 slots  — Pay: 100
```

---

**[VISUAL]** Half the dots quietly turn blue. Half turn coral. No announcement.

**[NARRATION]** "We also secretly assign everyone a gender. Men and women are completely identical in this simulation — same attribute distributions, same learning rates, same rules. Everything is equal."

**[VISUAL]** Side by side: male profile, female profile. Identical distributions.

**[NARRATION]** "But purely by chance — the same chance that gives you more heads than tails in fifty coin flips — most of the people who score highly in the job-relevant attributes happen to be men."

**[VISUAL]** Job A ring fills. All 15 dots are blue.

**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `Job A — 0% women. Before the simulation has run a single round.`

**[NARRATION]** "Not because of discrimination. Not because of biology. Just statistics and a starting distribution that slightly favours men in the attributes that happen to matter."

---

**[VISUAL]** Network lines form between dots.

**[NARRATION]** "Now — how do people make friends? For our first simulation: everyone becomes friends with the four people most similar to them. And those friendships stay forever."

**[VISUAL]** Lines solidify. Blue mostly connects to blue. Coral to coral. A handful of faint lines cross between groups.

**[NARRATION]** "People are also influenced by their job peers — the people they work alongside every day. Friends matter more: eighty percent of what shapes you comes from your social circle. Twenty percent comes from your coworkers."

**[VISUAL]** Two arrows converge on a single dot: `Friends 80%` and `Job peers 20%`.

**[NARRATION]** "One last ingredient. People aren't blank slates. Each person has a hidden set of innate tendencies — natural inclinations that aren't shaped by anyone. Completely random. No gender bias. They create a slow natural drift, pulling each person gradually toward who they actually are underneath."

**[VISUAL]** A faint inner glow briefly visible inside each dot — the innate profile — then recedes.

**[NARRATION]** "Every round: people influence each other, attributes update, jobs are reassigned. Let's run it."

**[VISUAL]** A simple loop graphic: Influence → Update → Reassign → repeat. Simulation begins. Iteration counter starts.

## PART 4 — SIMULATION 1: FIXED FRIENDS

**[VISUAL]** Simulation runs. Dots pulse faintly. Iteration counter ticks. The job rings barely change.

**[NARRATION]** "And... almost nothing happens."

**[VISUAL]** Pause. Before and after distributions shown side by side. Nearly identical.

**[NARRATION]** "This makes sense. Similar people become friends. Their attributes pull toward each other. They stay similar. They keep the same jobs. Nobody has any reason to move."

**[VISUAL]** The innate glow pulses back into visibility. Tiny drifts begin — people slowly diverging from their friends as innate tendencies assert themselves.

**[NARRATION]** "But innate tendencies create a slow drift. Over time, people become slightly different from who they were. And this changes something."

---

**[VISUAL]** Zoom into the boundary between blue and coral clusters. A single line crossing between them lights up in amber.

**[NARRATION]** "Look here. These two people were similar enough to become friends early on. Their attributes have drifted slightly since then — but the friendship held. It's permanent."

**[NARRATION]** "This is a bridge. And through this single connection, influence begins to cross the gap."

**[VISUAL]** A slow ripple emanates from the bridge — spreading hop by hop through the network in both directions.

**[NARRATION]** "It is slow. It depends on the accidental overlap of two people at the right moment. But over hundreds of iterations, more bridges appear as more people drift into each other's range. And the effects compound."

**[VISUAL]** Simulation resumes at speed. Amber bridge links flicker into existence over time. Coral dots gradually appear in the Job A ring.

**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `Iteration 300 — Women in Job A: 15%`
**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `Iteration 600 — Women in Job A: 33% | Female pay: 68% of male`

**[NARRATION]** "After six hundred rounds: one in three Job A positions held by a woman. The pay gap nearly halved. Progress — driven entirely by bridge links."

## PART 6 — SIMULATION 2: DYNAMIC FRIENDS AND ECHO CHAMBERS

**[VISUAL]** New simulation. Friendship lines flicker — forming and dissolving each round.

**[NARRATION]** "In the dynamic simulation, every round you make new friends with your most similar people — and let go of anyone who has drifted away."

**[VISUAL]** Fast-forward. Clusters tighten. Cross-gender lines flicker briefly and immediately dissolve.

**[NARRATION]** "In the fixed simulation, friendships formed early and held even as people changed. Those were bridges that couldn't be broken. In the dynamic simulation, the moment two people become different, the friendship dissolves. Bridges don't just stop forming. They actively collapse."

**[VISUAL]** A bridge link between a blue and coral dot brightens — then breaks cleanly and disappears.

**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `Iteration 600 — Women in Job A: 21% | Female pay: 43% of male`

**[NARRATION]** "Worse than the fixed case. A more socially active world produces more segregation — not because of intent, but because fluidity without any permanent anchor means echo chambers without end."

**[VISUAL]** Quick aside — a phone screen. Social media feed scrolling.

**[NARRATION]** "Social media is exactly this mechanism, made algorithmic. If you consume the same content as someone else, you get pulled into the same bubble. The similarity threshold is just a recommendation engine. Our world was already an echo chamber. Social media made it louder — and faster."

The bridges that might have carried her upward collapse before they can help."

## PART 7 — GENDER AWARENESS ATTEMPTS

### F-F Bias

**[VISUAL]** New simulation. Coral-coral connections form more easily. A dense female network emerges.

**[NARRATION]** "In this simulation, women are more likely to befriend other women. Maybe they don't trust male-dominated spaces. Maybe they're looking for role models who look like them."

**[VISUAL]** Female network becomes a tight, dense web. Cross-gender connections thin out.

**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `Iteration 600 — Women in Job A: 11% | Female pay: 35% of male`
**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `The worst outcome in the entire simulation.`

**[NARRATION]** "When women preferentially bond with women, their attributes converge toward the female average — which starts low in the job-relevant traits. Not because women are less capable. Aanya and Priya proved otherwise. But because the influence they exchange is shaped by the world they already occupy. A feedback loop: low traits lead to lower jobs, which reinforce lower traits."

**[VISUAL]** The tight female cluster — all trait bars similar, all low in the gold attributes. A feedback loop arrow animates.

**[NARRATION]** "Now — passive solidarity and organised solidarity are completely different things. Using a network to build real power, push for institutional change, create mentorship pipelines — that's worth pursuing, and this simulation doesn't test it. What this tests is the passive version: bonding for comfort. And bonding for comfort, inside a stratified system, only reinforces where you already are."

---

### Financial Ambition

**[VISUAL]** A thought bubble above a coral dot — she wants to grow her job attributes. An arrow pointing upward. Then the bubble dims.

**[NARRATION]** "What if women simply decided to pursue the rewarded traits more aggressively? The problem: financial ambition is itself one of the attributes being suppressed by the network. You can't want to grow what you've never had exposure to. And even if you do want it — you still need people to learn from. Which is exactly what the network has been blocking."

**[VISUAL]** Priya reaching upward — a lattice of friendship lines forms a ceiling above her.

---

### Reservation

**[VISUAL]** Two slots in Job A highlighted. Banner: `2 of 15 Job A slots reserved for women.`

**[NARRATION]** "What if we just force it? Reserve two of the fifteen Job A positions for the highest-scoring women every round."

**[VISUAL]** Two coral dots enter Job A at iteration 1. Then zoom in on one — her friendship lines trailing back into Job B and C. Blue dots surround her in Job A but no connections form.

**[NARRATION]** "She is in Job A. But her friends aren't here. Job peers only contribute twenty percent of trait growth. Without friends in her new environment, the influence that might consolidate her position barely reaches her."

**[VISUAL]** Long-run comparison: reservation curve vs no-reservation curve over 600 iterations. Both plateau at nearly identical levels.

**[TEXT ON SCREEN]**
```
No reservation:    Job A female — 21%   Pay: 43%
With reservation:  Job A female — 20%   Pay: 43%
(mean over 20 independent runs)
```

**[NARRATION]** "Across twenty independent runs, reservation barely changes where society settles. It shifts the starting point. It doesn't shift the destination."

**[NARRATION]** "There is also something this simulation can't fully capture but is worth naming: when someone earns a position, it's assumed they deserved it. When someone is placed there by policy, the assumption shifts. Did she get here on merit, or for optics? Reservation carries the risk of permanently framing achievement as suspect — making things harder for the very people it's meant to help. In a world with real outright discrimination, reservation may still be necessary. Under these conditions, a small reservation alone doesn't change the equilibrium."

## PART 8 — RELATIONSHIPS

**[VISUAL]** A blue dot and a coral dot drift toward each other. Screen splits: left = *Positive*, right = *Negative.*

**[NARRATION]** "What about relationships? In most of the world, relationships are heterosexual — one of the most common points of direct cross-gender influence. Whether that helps or hurts depends entirely on the nature of the relationship."

**[VISUAL — LEFT]** Trait values flowing both directions. Both attributes grow. Her job-relevant bars climb.

**[NARRATION]** "In a positive relationship, two different people genuinely learn from each other. Difference becomes an asset. This kind of relationship is a genuinely powerful equaliser."

**[VISUAL — RIGHT]** Division of labour calcifies. His domestic traits flatline. Her job-relevant traits plateau, then slowly decline.

**[NARRATION]** "In a negative dynamic — the one that dating culture has long normalised — roles get assigned rather than explored. Division of labour that feels practical in the short term slowly removes the incentive to develop traits that would otherwise grow. Being with someone who handles financial ambition can, in the wrong dynamic, become a substitute for having it yourself. Same relationship. Two entirely different outcomes. Depending on whether difference is treated as something to learn from — or something to divide."

## PART 9 — THE ANSWER

**[VISUAL]** Reset. All 100 dots scattered. Friendship lines begin forming — randomly. Each person's four friends drawn from anywhere in the population. No similarity calculation.

**[NARRATION]** "So what actually changes the equilibrium?"

**[NARRATION]** "The answer is diversity. But you can't mandate diversity in a dynamic world — people will always drift toward the similar if given the choice. The question is when and how you get the diversity in."

**[VISUAL]** The random network solidifies. Blue and coral thoroughly mixed. Lines crossing in every direction.

**[NARRATION]** "What if your first four friends weren't the people most like you — but simply whoever you happened to meet? And what if those friendships lasted — not necessarily forever, but long enough to survive the drift. Long enough to keep the bridge open even after you've changed."

**[VISUAL]** Fast-forward simulation. Coral dots enter Job A steadily, earlier than in any previous scenario.

**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `Iteration 100 — Women in Job A: 28%`
**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `Iteration 300 — Women in Job A: 49%`
**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** `Iteration 600 — Women in Job A: 52% | Female pay: 105% of male`

**[NARRATION]** "No reservation. No gender policy. No gender awareness. Four random friends, held long enough to matter. That's it."

**[VISUAL]** Aanya's dot found in Job B at the start. Her random friends include people from across the population.

**[NARRATION]** "Aanya eventually reaches Job A — not because she happened to get the right connections, but because in a world of genuinely mixed networks, high innate potential finds its way to the surface consistently. Run this twenty times. It holds."

**[VISUAL]** 20-run bar chart. Random childhood at ~52% female in Job A. All other scenarios well below.

## PART 10 — PROBLEMS WITH THE SIMULATION

**[VISUAL]** The simulation fades. A list appears on screen, item by item.

**[NARRATION]** "Before you take any of this too seriously — let's be honest about what this simulation leaves out."

**[NARRATION]** "In the real world, women have very good reasons not to trust men as easily as men trust each other. That asymmetry shapes networks in ways we haven't modelled. Hiring doesn't work by meritocratic ranking — it often happens through networks from which women are structurally excluded. There is overt discrimination. There is harassment. There are institutional barriers that have existed for centuries."

**[VISUAL]** Clean text list. No dramatisation.

**[NARRATION]** "This simulation models none of that. And yet even in this sanitised, generous version of the problem — where everything is fair except the initial starting distribution and the shape of the network — the inequality is almost impossible to fight."

**[VISUAL]** All scenario results shown in one chart. Most barely moved.

**[NARRATION]** "In reality, everything we left out sits on top of everything we just showed. The simulation isn't an argument that things are fine. It's an argument that even the minimal version of the problem is this hard. The real thing is harder. The point of building this world from scratch was to show that you don't need malice, you don't need intent, you don't even need anyone to be aware of gender — for a patriarchy to emerge and persist."

## PART 11 — BEHAVIOURAL SCIENCE IS TRASH

**[VISUAL]** A stack of academic papers. Contradictory titles side by side.

**[NARRATION]** "The only reason we built a simulation in the first place is that the actual science on this topic is not reliable enough to settle the question."

**[NARRATION]** "Half the studies disagree with each other. The other half disagree with themselves. The results are heavily influenced by the political context in which they're produced — which, yes, someone wrote a paper on. Papers never lie."

**[VISUAL]** A timeline of behavioural science. It starts shockingly recently.

**[NARRATION]** "Think about how young this field is. Sigmund Freud — who famously claimed infant girls were attracted to their fathers, among other things — only died about eighty years ago. While still a respected academic. It's quite poetic that we understood quantum mechanics before we ever began to understand a woman."

**[VISUAL]** Freud portrait. A physics equation. A confused face.

**[NARRATION]** "To be fair to Freud — cocaine was still legal. That probably explains some of it."

---

**[NARRATION]** "Here is what a real experiment on this question would actually require. You would need to raise over a hundred children in a completely controlled environment — controlled influences, controlled social rules — and monitor them for thirty or more years. Only then could you begin to separate what is biological from what is social."

**[VISUAL]** A controlled research facility. A thirty-year timeline stretching out. The scale of it visualised.

**[NARRATION]** "That experiment has never been run. It almost certainly never will be."

**[NARRATION]** "What this simulation tells us is not whether men and women are biologically capable of equal outcomes. Maybe they are. Maybe there are real differences we haven't accounted for. We genuinely don't know. What it does tell us: even if we are biologically capable of full equality — our society simply won't allow it. Not yet. Not with the shape of the networks we currently live inside."

---

## PART 12 — GENDER INEQUALITY PARADOX

**[VISUAL]** World map. More gender-equal countries highlighted. A bar chart appears: larger occupational gender gaps in those same countries.

**[NARRATION]** "There is one more thing this simulation actually explains — something that often gets used as an argument against gender equality."

**[NARRATION]** "The gender equality paradox: in more gender-equal countries, men and women choose more different occupations, not fewer. If opportunity is equal, why does the gap get larger?"

**[NARRATION]** "Our simulation answers this directly. A so-called gender-equal society isn't a society where influences are equal — it's a society where people are free to follow their conditioned preferences without economic coercion forcing them otherwise. A poorer society may force both its most talented men and women into the highest-paying roles out of necessity. A richer, freer society lets people follow their interests — which, after decades of different socialisation, happen to diverge."

**[NARRATION]** "This is what happens when you achieve equality of choice without equality of influence. The paradox doesn't say anything about biology. It says that male-coded jobs still pay more than female-coded ones — and that free societies are better at revealing the gap between how men and women have been shaped, not what they're capable of."

---

## PART 13 — CONCLUSION

**[VISUAL]** A clean graph. X-axis: time (0 to 600 iterations). Y-axis: female pay as a percentage of male, starting at 30%. A curve rises, then flattens — plateauing around 43%. Label: *Baseline.*

**[NARRATION]** "Here is what the pay gap looks like over time in this simulation. It rises — then it plateaus. Society finds an equilibrium and sits there."

**[VISUAL]** A second curve overlays: reservation scenario. Starts slightly higher, rises a little faster early, converges to the same plateau.

**[NARRATION]** "Systemic interventions — reservation, targeted training, support programmes — can help during the growth phase. They move the curve upward. They reduce the time it takes to get there. They are worth doing."

**[VISUAL]** Label on the reservation curve: *Systemic change.* Arrow pointing to the early section: *Helpful here.*

**[NARRATION]** "But they don't change the plateau. They don't shift where the system settles."

**[VISUAL]** The random childhood curve appears. It doesn't plateau. It keeps climbing, crossing 100%, settling near parity. Label: *Individual change.*

**[NARRATION]** "What changes the equilibrium are individual changes. Being genuinely open to people who are different from you. Seeking out experiences outside your natural circle — without judgement. Being a positive influence in your relationships rather than a mirror."

---

**[NARRATION]** "A lot of people will argue that you simply cannot bring about individual change at the scale of billions of people. That structural problems need structural solutions, and expecting personal behaviour to move the needle is naive."

**[VISUAL]** Slow zoom out on the graph. All curves visible together.

**[NARRATION]** "But look at how much the world has changed in the last fifty years. Look at what your parents' generation considered normal. Aren't we already different from them? And isn't it reasonable to think our children might be different from us — growing up in more diversity, with more exposure, with more of these conversations already in the air?"

**[NARRATION]** "Saying it will never happen doesn't make it less necessary. It just makes it less likely."

**[VISUAL]** Fade to black. One line of text appears.

**[TEXT ON SCREEN]** *"I would rather imagine something that fails than fail to imagine anything at all."*

**[VISUAL]** Hold. Fade out.
